## 1. The mirror

Put an experiment in front of a mirror. Would the reflection obey the same laws?

For a long time, physicists expected that it would. In 1956, Lee and Yang pointed out that this expectation had not been properly tested for the weak interaction. Chien-Shiung Wu and her collaborators made the test.

Working with the National Bureau of Standards team, Wu aligned cobalt nuclei and watched the electrons emitted in beta decay. The electrons preferred one direction relative to the nuclear spin.

[Reverse momentum under parity; leave the axial spin arrow unchanged. Hold.]

The mirror changes that relationship. The weak interaction distinguishes the two arrangements.

Matter has a handedness in its interactions. Could that fact help explain its structure?

## 2. A symmetry you can move

We need a way to talk about symmetry that goes beyond the shape of an object.

Turn this marked sphere around one axis, then another. Now reverse the order.

[Compare the endpoints.]

The result changes. Make both turns very small and the difference becomes small too, but it has a definite mathematical form.

A Lie group describes continuous transformations. Its Lie algebra describes their infinitesimal generators and how they combine. The bracket records the effect of changing their order.

In particle physics, the transformations we need often act on internal fields, rather than rotating an object in ordinary space.

## 3. What a representation means

Here is one transformation: turn a phase through an angle.

This field's arrow turns once. Another turns twice. The symmetry is the same; its action on the fields is different.

Those different transformation rules are representations.

Under the weak SU(2) symmetry, the left-chiral electron belongs to a two-component representation with its neutrino. The right-chiral electron is a singlet under that symmetry.

The word “two” counts independent complex components. This animation shows how field components transform, not an electron physically changing into a neutrino.

## 4. Why mass enters the story

A Dirac mass couples left- and right-chiral fields. For the electron, their different electroweak representations prevent us from writing that coupling on its own while preserving the gauge symmetry.

The Higgs field supplies the missing transformation. The complete interaction is invariant, and the Higgs background gives the electron a mass.

A massive electron still has chiral weak interactions. And chirality is not the same as helicity, the alignment of spin with momentum, for a massive particle.

The useful point is specific: transformation rules constrain which mass terms are allowed.

## 5. A second constraint

The quantum theory imposes another test.

Gauge symmetry allows different mathematical descriptions of the same physical situation. Those descriptions must continue to agree after quantum corrections.

A gauge anomaly obstructs that consistency. In four dimensions, a local anomaly calculation for chiral fermions contains a cubic expression in the gauge generators.

Think of a tensor with three input slots. Give it three symmetry directions and it produces a number. The representation determines this tensor.

The question is no longer just which transformations the matter admits. It is which ones survive this quantum consistency test.

## 6. What we normally choose

Grand unification has found remarkably economical ways to organise a family.

SU(5) uses a ten and a conjugate five. Spin(10) fits a family and a right-handed neutrino into a single sixteen. E₆ places them in a twenty-seven with additional components.

These choices of group do not, by themselves, fix the number of repetitions.

Other constructions tie the answer to geometry. In a heterotic standard embedding, the net number of families depends on an index of the compactification. The same exceptional branching can appear there, but its three-dimensional SU(3) factor is not automatically three four-dimensional families.

This paper asks a different question. Can we vary the symmetry and its matter representation together, and classify everything that satisfies specified conditions?

## 7. Geometry supplies a candidate

Return to the sphere and mark its north pole.

Some rotations leave that point fixed. Call their group H. The larger rotation group is G. Directions that move the point give its tangent plane.

H acts on that plane. So the embedding has supplied a representation of its own.

The general construction is the quotient of the two Lie algebras: q equals g over h.

The paper starts directly with effective proper inclusions of compact real Lie algebras. No nonzero ideal of g may lie inside h. The entire adjoint complement must be irreducible of complex type.

Irreducible means there is no smaller nonzero subspace preserved by every symmetry transformation. On our tangent plane, write z equals x plus i y. Under rotation, z and its conjugate acquire opposite phases.

For a representation of complex type, the complexification splits into two inequivalent conjugate halves. Choose one half as the internal representation for left-handed Weyl matter. We have not cut the physical plane into two pieces.

This is the algebraic starting point. The compact homogeneous space is a realization of the qualifying pair, obtained after the structural reduction. The algebraic conditions are motivated by physics, not conclusions of Wu's experiment.

## 8. Let the tensor find the ideal

Now hold one input of the cubic tensor fixed. Let the other two range over the whole isotropy algebra.

If every result is zero, the fixed direction lies in the radical.

[Move the two free inputs independently; hold the universal quantifier on screen.]

All means all, including mixed insertions. This matters while a centre is allowed. Once the algebra is semisimple, mixed cubic terms between its simple factors vanish, and the pure and mixed tests agree for an ideal.

The radical is automatically an ideal. The paper requires it to be nonzero alongside complex type. Both conditions enter the structural reduction; the radical condition helps exclude a centre.

Our sphere example fails: its charged complex half has a nonzero U(1) cubic. A picture that helped us understand the construction need not satisfy the theorem's conditions.

## 9. The alternatives fall away

The proof first reduces the structure. In particular, a central U(1) in the isotropy algebra makes the radical vanish through mixed terms.

It then separates equal-rank and lower-rank possibilities. The lower-rank branch is excluded by index arithmetic and highest-weight arguments. The classical argument covers arbitrary rank.

The equal-rank branch reaches six terminal candidates.

[The six pairs appear; retain the proof map as context.]

For these semisimple isotropy algebras, mixed cubic terms between distinct simple factors vanish because the generators act tracelessly. Each factor has its own cubic coefficient. A zero coefficient puts that factor in the radical. Opposite signs on different factors do not cancel: these are different components of the tensor.

Five rows have no zero coefficient. Their radicals vanish.

One row remains. E₈ contains E₆ plus a commuting su(3), and the complex half is twenty-seven tensor three. The E₆ coefficient is zero. The other coefficient is twenty-seven.

The radical is exactly E₆.

The commuting SU(3) is treated as a background global symmetry. Its cubic anomaly remains; gauging it would require additional anomaly cancellation.

This is a uniqueness and no-go theorem in the stated domain: the pair works, and every inequivalent alternative fails.

## 10. A second classification

The next question is inside E₆.

Its maximal proper closed root subsystems form three Weyl orbits. Choose one subsystem from each orbit: twenty-seven choices, thirty-six choices, forty choices.

That gives thirty-eight thousand, eight hundred and eighty triples. Up to the simultaneous symmetry action, there are six kinds.

The paper identifies the pairwise-maximal and common-maximal kinds. These are different extrema. Exactly these two have a common A₂ component, which becomes colour along the reconstructed chains.

For each triple, form the pairwise derived groups. Select the one of greatest dimension, then intersect it with the third regular group. The pairwise-maximal kind gives the Standard Model group; the common-maximal kind gives the left–right group. Compatible representatives fit within a common chain.

This operation matters. An ordinary three-way overlap is not the construction.

The global group forms and the hypercharge combination are then recovered, including the finite identifications that the Lie algebra alone cannot show.

## 11. Count the matter

Look again at twenty-seven tensor three.

Restricted to E₆, it is three copies of the twenty-seven. The multiplicity comes from the commuting factor.

Under the selected embedding, each twenty-seven contains one minimal chiral family, a five and conjugate five, and two singlets.

The conjugate pair contributes zero to the net chiral class. The singlets do too. They remain in the full representation.

The net result is three copies of the Standard Model family class, up to conjugation. This follows by restricting the selected representation and taking its chiral class.

## 12. Return to matter

The result begins with a physical question and answers a precise mathematical one.

Within this compact algebraic domain, the symmetry and its canonical representation cannot be chosen freely once the two conditions are imposed. One pair remains. A second classification recovers the Standard Model structure along the specified construction.

Wu's experiment showed that a mirror could reveal something the laws of nature distinguish. The question here is how far the mathematics of that distinction, together with the cubic condition and an adjoint-complement origin, can take us.
